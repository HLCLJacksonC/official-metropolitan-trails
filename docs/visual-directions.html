Metropolitan Trails · Shanghai · visual direction review

Three ways to draw the same walk

One trail, rendered three times, so a direction can be picked before the map and the entry forms get built. Same six stops, same route geometry, same field notes — only the visual system changes. Each board also shows the parts that have to survive contact with the app: a theme filter, a stop label, and one pin opened.

Reportage. Halftone, three colours, typewriter labels on leader lines, and a cross that marks where the route stops being a choice.
Good for
The argument. It is the only direction with a way to draw friction — the detour, the gate, the fence — which is what the project is actually recording.

Recommendation

Don't pick one. These three aren't competing styles for the same screen — they're each strongest at a different one of the six requirements, and the project has three distinct surfaces to build.

Map view + filters ····· A, at lower saturation
Trail narrative view ··· B's numbered strip, C's typography
Pin detail ············ C
House palette ········· C

C carries the identity. It's the only one of the three whose visual language has a word for obstruction. The waterworks detour at stop 2 is the clearest finding on this whole walk — a promenade that lifts you onto a bridge so you pass the 1883 building without ever standing next to it — and A renders that as a wiggle in a line while C marks it. For a project whose premise is that the map smooths over what the body notices, the direction that can draw the friction is the direction that agrees with the research.

B's structure survives, its warmth doesn't. Take the numbered stop strip and the legend-that-teaches-its-own-notation straight into the narrative view. Leave the rag paper and the drawn landmarks: an interpretive-panel look tells the reader the city has already been explained to them, which is the opposite of the claim.

A becomes the index, not the identity. Drop the saturation by about half and it's a good all-trails map — the stats block gives each walk a face at small size, and it is the only direction that stays readable with eight routes on one screen. It should look like C's quieter sibling, not its rival.

The lowest-effort path: build in C's palette and type, use the B strip for the story view, and treat A as a display mode of the same map rather than a second design system.
