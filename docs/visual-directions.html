Metropolitan Trails · Shanghai · visual direction review

Three ways to draw the same walk

One trail, rendered three times, so a direction can be picked before the map and the entry forms get built. Same six stops, same route geometry, same field notes — only the visual system changes. Each board also shows the parts that have to survive contact with the app: a theme filter, a stop label, and one pin opened.

Data-poster

Reads as
A record of a walk that was measured. Flat saturated ground, the city reduced to a ghost grid, one route that refuses to be missed.
Good for
The map view and any cross-trail comparison — six of these side by side stay legible, and the stats give each walk an identity.
Strains at
Photographs and long reflections. The poster wants numbers; the project's substance is text and images.

Direction B

Night network

Reads as
The whole city drawn in full on a black ground, with one route lit. Nothing is simplified away — the density is the argument.
Good for
The map view at any zoom, and the poster. The street grid is a real object here, so a walk that cuts against it reads as a fact rather than a claim.
Strains at
Long text. There is nowhere quiet to put a paragraph, so the reading has to happen in a panel beside the map, never on it.
B · street network built as rotated district grids — the same footprints the 3D massing will stand on.

Editorial diagram

Reads as
Reportage. Halftone, three colours, typewriter labels on leader lines, and a cross that marks where the route stops being a choice.
Good for
The argument. It is the only direction with a way to draw friction — the detour, the gate, the fence — which is what the project is actually recording.
Strains at
Being a plan. It naturally becomes an elevation, as below; a top-down map in this language needs a separate solution.
C · drawn as a section along the river rather than a plan — the waterfront read at eye level, west to east.

Recommendation

B carries it. The night network is the only one of the three where the city is drawn in full rather than summarised, and that matters more here than it would on most projects: the claim is that the promenade smooths over what the body notices, and you cannot make that claim on a map that has already smoothed the city into six dots and a line. Every street is there; the route is the one thing lit. Everything else in the system should be built to serve that board.

C stays, as the reading surface. Not as a rival direction — as the annotation grammar B doesn’t have room for. The ✗ marks are already in B because the idea is too central to leave out, but the boxed leader-line labels and the west-to-east elevation belong on the trail page, where a stop gets a paragraph and a photograph. Its vermilion becomes the magenta; nothing else changes.

A is now redundant. It is B in daylight — same composition, same single-route logic, less conviction. Keep it only if you need a light or print variant, and if you do, rebuild it as a white-ground version of B rather than maintaining a second palette.

On the 2D/3D question. The network here is generated as sixteen rotated district grids, which means the footprints already exist as data. The 3D pass is one transform on that same structure — extrude each district’s blocks at a fixed axonometric angle, light them from one side, and leave the route flat and magenta on top so it stays the brightest thing on the board. The elevation on C is the other half of the same idea, drawn by hand. Neither needs the 2D work redone.

Next, if you want it: swap the procedural street grid for real Yangshupu geometry, and build the same board at three zooms to confirm the density survives.
